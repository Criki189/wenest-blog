It's Saturday morning. You're looking out the kitchen window with a coffee, and the lawn looks like it survived a skirmish. There's a bald patch near the trampoline, a dead strip by the side path, and the whole back corner is thinning out. 

By Sunday evening, you will know exactly why those bare spots appeared and how to fix them before the next round of Sydney weather makes it worse.

## Diagnose the patch before you touch the soil

If you throw seed or turf on a bare spot without knowing why the grass died, you are just setting a trap for the new grass. Most patchy lawns in Sydney fail for one of four reasons: compaction, grubs, pet urine, or thatch. You need to figure out which one is staring back at you.

Compaction is the usual suspect in high-traffic areas. If the bare patch is right where the kids run to the clothesline, or where the wheelie bin sits, the soil is probably as hard as concrete. Water runs off it before it reaches the roots.

Grubs are the other major offender. Pull gently on the brown grass near a patch. If it lifts away like a carpet with zero resistance, you have army grub or lawn grub damage. They chew through the roots and leave the top layer dead. 

Did a job in Pennant Hills last November — four-bed Federation home, beautiful Kikuyu lawn, completely wrecked in three weeks. The owners thought it was the drought. We dug down two centimetres and found more grubs than soil. If you have dogs, their urine is high in nitrogen and concentrated to a single spot, burning the grass. 

Thatch is a spongy layer of dead organic matter sitting above the soil. A bit of thatch is fine, but Buffalo grass builds it up fast. Too much and water cannot penetrate the ground. Check the soil moisture under the thatch before you water next time; it is parched.

## Aerate to break up the compaction

If your soil is compacted, aerating is step one. It is not a glamorous job, but the results are fast.

You can use a sturdy garden fork for small patches. Drive the tines in about 75mm deep and lever it back slightly to fracture the clay. Move backwards across the patch in 100mm intervals. For anything larger than a few square metres, hire a core aerator from the local hardware store for half a day. Pulling plugs of soil out of the ground is rough on the lawn for a few days, but it is the best way to get oxygen and water down into the root zone.

Leave the soil plugs on the lawn. They look terrible for a day. Then they break down and return nutrients to the soil. If your clay is particularly heavy, a few weeks after aerating you can top-dress with a sandy loam to improve drainage going forward.

## Top-dress to level and feed

Top-dressing fixes minor depressions in the ground and gives your grass a fresh layer of growing medium. It is especially useful for [Buffalo grass repair](/) because the runners sit above the ground and need contact with soil to put down new roots.

First, mow your lawn. If you leave it long, the top-dressing will bury the grass and kill it.

Buy a sandy loam or a specific underlay soil from your local landscape supplier. Do not use cheap topsoil; it is often full of weeds. Shovel the soil over the bare patches, then use a stiff broom or the back of a rake to sweep it into the low spots and the aeration holes. 

Aim for a layer no more than 10mm to 20mm deep. You want the blades of grass still poking through. Give it a quick soak. Do not flood it, which causes runoff.

## Patching the bare spots

This is where you actually fix the dead patches. The method depends on what grass you have.

For Buffalo or Kikuyu, the fastest and most reliable way is to plug the patch with turf. Buy a slab of the same variety from your turf supplier, cut it to size with a sharp knife, and drop it in. Make sure it sits flush with the surrounding soil; raised turf edges will get scalped by your mower later and scalped turf dies quickly.

If the bare patch is a urine mark from the dog, soak the soil first to flush the nitrogen, then lay the new turf. Water it in and keep it moist for a fortnight.

For a seed repair, which works well for cool-season grasses like ryegrass or fescue, scratch the soil surface with a rake, scatter the seed, and cover it with about 5mm of topsoil or sugar cane mulch. Keep it damp. Honestly, trying to grow Buffalo from seed is a nightmare nobody has time for; just buy the turf and save your weekends.

### The right watering schedule for new turf

Water the patch twice a day for the first week. Morning and afternoon is fine, avoiding the heat of the middle of the day. 

After two weeks, pull a corner of the turf gently. If it resists, the roots have taken. It works. Sometimes too well, and you will be mowing it within a month. Back off the watering to once a day, then gradually taper down. Establishing deep roots early means your lawn will survive the next harsh Sydney summer.

## The Wenest take

In the homes we work in across Sydney, the version of this that actually fails is the weekend warrior who throws an inch of topsoil over a heavily compacted, grub-infested patch and expects the lawn to save itself. The soil just caps it over, locking the dead roots in place, and the bare spot gets bigger. The fix is always diagnosis first. Treat the pests and frack the clay open before you spend a dollar on new turf. 

Long-term, following a proper lawn care schedule in Sydney means feeding the grass at the start of spring and autumn. A wetting agent is genuinely valuable here. Sydney water hardness in the Inner West leaves a waxy build-up in the soil profile, meaning water runs off instead of wetting the dirt. A soil wetter breaks that surface tension and pulls the water down. Apply it in early spring before the heat hits, which is one of the easiest things you can do without hiring gear. 

We have never gotten a straight answer out of the chemical companies on exactly why some premium wetters last six months and others wash away in three weeks, but the ones with surfactant seem to stick around longer.
